How to read a parcel in Camposcope, one layer at a time

One PID from ParcelMap BC is enough for Camposcope to draw the parcel, ring it with three bands of neighbourhood and pull fifteen years of crop inventory and twenty-five of forest and fire records. This walkthrough opens a real orchard parcel in Kelowna in the order the app presents it, and shows where each number comes from, what it means and where it stops.

1

From a PID to a polygon

The search box takes a PID, an address, a municipality or a coordinate. The PID, the nine digits on a tax notice or a title, is the shortest route: Camposcope asks ParcelMap BC, the Province's parcel fabric, for that one parcel. One layer covers the whole province, and the app only ever asks it for one parcel, one point or one municipality at a time.

In the app: the Search box on the Canada page, or a direct link with /canada?pid=. Clicking the map also selects the parcel under the click.

2

What the fabric says, and what it does not

ParcelMap BC is a surveyed fabric, so a parcel's boundary was drawn from legal plans, not traced by its owner. That makes it the opposite of Brazil's self-declared rural registry, and Camposcope still shows its fields exactly as they arrive. It never names an owner: the fabric says only whether the parcel is private, Crown or another kind.

In the app: the Parcel card. When a click lands on stacked parcels (strata, air space), the app lists them all and lets you choose.

3

Three bands out from the property line

On its own, a parcel cannot tell you whether its history is common or rare in the valley. So every analysis also runs in three bands around it: up to 500 m, 500 m to 2 km, and 2 to 5 km. They are measured outward from the boundary, not from a centre, and none overlaps another, so no area is counted twice.

In the app: the Zones section, and the Parcel / 0–500 m / 500 m–2 km / 2–5 km buttons above every chart.

4

Fifteen crop inventories in one chart

Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada's Annual Crop Inventory maps every 30 m pixel of the country's farmland, one year at a time, down to the crop: orchards, vineyards, hay, corn. Each column is one year, from 2011, when the inventory first covered all of Canada, to the latest.

In the app: the Land cover tab. The map follows the tab and shows the year chosen in the selector.

5

The parcel against its neighbourhood

The same series, now as the share of each zone that is natural vegetation: forest, wetland, grassland and shrub. If the parcel and the bands move together, the change was regional. If the parcel breaks away from its neighbourhood, the story is its own.

In the app: the Land cover tab with the percent switch, changing zones. Here the map shows the first year of the series, to compare with the previous step.

6

Where every hectare came from

The column chart says how much of each class there was. The flow diagram says what became what, pixel by pixel, between the first and the last year. The pale band joining a class to itself is what stayed the same.

In the app: the Transitions tab, where you choose the two years and compare the maps side by side.

7

Twenty-five years of tree-cover loss

Since 2001, Hansen/GLAD has flagged every year the 30 m pixels that lost their tree cover, to logging, fire, pests or building. When the fabric records a parcel's start date, Camposcope splits the loss at it; most older parcels have none, and then the series stays whole, with a note, rather than being split at an invented date.

In the app: the Forest tab, which also shows stand age from the national forest inventory (NTEMS).

8

Biomass: a model, with a larger error

Above-ground biomass comes from ESA CCI, in 100 m pixels and for ten years between 2007 and 2022. It is not a measurement on the parcel: it is a radar-and-lidar model calibrated at global scale, with a large error per pixel. Use it for trends and for comparing zones, not for a carbon inventory.

In the app: the Biomass tab, in megagrams per hectare (Mg/ha) by zone.

9

Fire, at a coarser scale

Canada's own burned-area maps are not in Earth Engine, so the fire record comes from NASA's MODIS (MCD64A1), month by month since 2001, in 500 m pixels of about 21 ha. A fire smaller than that does not register, and on a small parcel the app says so instead of reporting a confident zero.

In the app: the Fire tab, with history, frequency and year of the last fire for each zone.

10

Two maps, two answers

The crop inventory is built to see farms; VLCE2, from the Canadian Forest Service, is built to see forests. Camposcope sets one against the other, grouped into the same broad classes, pixel by pixel. Where they disagree, it is usually because one of them has no word for what is on the ground.

In the app: the Validation tab, with the agreement matrix for each zone.

11

Where every number came from

Each analysis keeps its source, version, scale and time of calculation. All of that travels with the PDF report and the exported spreadsheet, because a challenged number only holds if it can be reproduced.

In the app: the Download data button (an ODS spreadsheet with one sheet per table) and the parcel's PDF report.

What Camposcope does not say

  • It does not say who owns the land. The fabric publishes the parcel and its owner type, never a name, and Camposcope adds nothing to that. Title belongs to the Land Title and Survey Authority.
  • It does not judge land use against zoning or the ALR. No screen says whether a use is permitted; that sits with the municipality and the Agricultural Land Commission.
  • It does not date a planting. A class that appears in the crop inventory may be a new orchard or an old one the inventory has learned to see; the satellite record alone cannot tell them apart.
  • It cannot see below 30 m, or 500 m for fire. On a 12 ha parcel every edge pixel weighs a lot, and a small fire may not register at all.

Notes

  1. The three UmaTerra tools differ in who draws the boundary they analyse: the state (Indigenous lands and conservation units), the analyst (a point on the map) or the landholder (Brazil's CAR). Camposcope's first premise is that the boundary is data to be examined, not input to be trusted. A surveyed fabric earns more trust than a self-declared one, and the app still shows registered against measured area rather than assuming they agree. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  2. Camposcope's design record explains why it does not use circles around a centre, as Naturametrics does: “A 40 000 ha property and a 12 ha one have nothing comparable about their centroids; ‘the first 500 m outside the fence’ means the same thing for both.” Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  3. The app refuses transitions between years less than five years apart. Over short gaps, most apparent change is classification flicker in mixed pixels: map error, not change on the ground. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  4. No code path writes a number without its provenance record. Every area on this page is a pixel count times the true pixel area, measured on the same grid as the count: Canada spans 41° to 83° N, and a flat 0.09 ha per pixel, or a grid mismatch, drifts by a third at Kelowna's latitude. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
