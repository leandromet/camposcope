How to read a rural property in Camposcope, one layer at a time

One code from Brazil's Rural Environmental Registry (CAR) is enough for Camposcope to draw the property, ring it with three bands of neighbourhood and pull forty years of satellite imagery. This walkthrough opens a real property in the order the app presents it, and shows where each number comes from, what it means and where it stops.

1

From a code to a polygon

Everything starts at the search box, which accepts a CAR code, a coordinate, a municipality or a place name, in that order. The code is the shortest route: its first two letters name the state, and SICAR's GeoServer looks the property up in that state's layer. There is no single national layer.

In the app: the Search box at the top of the sidebar, or a direct link with /?car=. Clicking the map also selects whatever property lies there.

2

What the registry says, and who says it

The polygon, the area and the status were all entered by the landholder. So Camposcope shows those fields exactly as they arrive: it does not colour the condition green or red, or turn the status into a badge. A CAR record proves neither ownership, nor possession, nor environmental compliance.

Overlapping registrations are normal in a self-declared system. The app lists each one, with the area in common, and never merges them into a single polygon.

In the app: the Property → Registry details panel. Overlapping neighbours appear when a click falls on more than one registration.

3

Three bands out from the fence line

On its own, a property cannot tell you whether its history is common or rare in the region. So every analysis also runs in three bands around it: up to 500 m, 500 m to 2 km, and 2 to 5 km. They are measured outward from the boundary, not from a centre, and none overlaps another, so no area is counted twice.

In the app: the Zones section of the sidebar, and the Property / 0–500 m / 500 m–2 km / 2–5 km buttons above every chart.

4

Forty years in one chart

Each column is one MapBiomas year, from 1985 to the latest, with the property's hectares in each land-cover class. The orange line marks the CAR registration, the only date on the chart that belongs to the property rather than to the satellite.

In the app: the Land cover tab. The map follows the tab and shows the year chosen in the selector.

5

The property against its neighbourhood

The same series, now as the share of each zone that is still native vegetation (forest, savanna and grassland). If the property and the bands fall together, the change was regional. If the property breaks away from its neighbourhood, the story is its own.

In the app: the Land cover tab with the percent switch, changing zones. Here the map shows the first year of the series, to compare with the previous step.

6

Where every hectare came from

The column chart says how much of each class there was. The flow diagram says what became what, pixel by pixel, between the first and the last year. The pale band joining a class to itself is what stayed the same.

In the app: the Transitions tab, where you choose the two years (or several stages) and compare the maps side by side.

7

Forest loss in three periods

Since 2001, Hansen/GLAD has flagged every year the 30 m pixels that lost their tree cover. Camposcope splits that loss into three periods that matter under the law: up to 2008, the Forest Code's cut-off; from 2008 to the CAR registration; and after registration.

One caveat: Hansen records no month, so the 22 July 2008 cut-off falls on the turn of the year, and forest gain is a single, undated flag.

In the app: the Forest tab. On the map, red is loss since 2008.

8

Biomass: a model, with a larger error

Above-ground biomass comes from ESA CCI, in 100 m pixels and for only ten years between 2007 and 2022. It is not a measurement on the property: it is a radar-and-lidar model calibrated at global scale, with a large error per pixel. Use it for trends and for comparing zones, not for a carbon inventory.

In the app: the Biomass tab, in megagrams per hectare (Mg/ha) by zone.

9

Forty years of fire

MapBiomas Fire maps burn scars year by year in 30 m pixels. Each column is the share of the property that burned that year. On the map, colour shows how many times each pixel has burned since 1985.

In the app: the Fire tab, with history, frequency and year of the last fire for each zone.

10

The 2008 image matters for its dates

Under the Forest Code, land already in use on 22 July 2008 counts as a consolidated rural area. The 2008 SPOT mosaic is the only detailed imagery (5 to 10 m) from that time, but each patch of it was photographed on a different day — in some regions after the cut-off, and in others never.

That is why Camposcope always shows the image with its dates, and never computes a consolidated area.

In the app: the SPOT 2008 layer and the Validation tab, with the SPOT × MapBiomas 2008 swipe comparison.

11

Two maps, two answers

IBGE's vegetation map is made of polygons drawn at regional scale; MapBiomas classifies every 30 m pixel every year. When they disagree, the disagreement is information too: usually IBGE still draws forest where MapBiomas has already seen cropland.

In the app: the Validation tab, with the IBGE × MapBiomas matrix and the swipe comparison on the map.

12

Where every number came from

Each analysis keeps its source, version, scale and time of calculation. All of that travels with the PDF report and the exported spreadsheet, because a challenged number only holds if it can be reproduced.

In the app: the Download data button (an ODS spreadsheet with one sheet per table) and the property's PDF report, with the same charts as this page.

What Camposcope does not say

  • It does not judge compliance. No screen says whether the property meets the Forest Code, must restore a legal reserve, or cleared with a permit. That judgement belongs to the environmental agency, with the case file in hand.
  • It does not compute consolidated area. The 2008 image and the loss-by-period split help tell the story; the legal classification depends on dates the satellite does not have.
  • It does not check the registry. Area, condition and status are the landholder's, exactly as SICAR publishes them (in Portuguese).
  • It does not show permanent preservation areas (APP) or legal reserves. SICAR publishes only the property outline; the inner layers sit behind a captcha, one municipality at a time. The bands on this page are neighbourhood, not APP.
  • It cannot see below 30 m. On small properties every edge pixel weighs a lot: 1 ha is about 11 pixels.

Notes

  1. The three UmaTerra tools differ in who draws the boundary they analyse: the state (Indigenous lands and conservation units), the analyst (a point on the map) or the landholder (the CAR). A CAR boundary is self-declared, and Camposcope's first premise is that the boundary is data to be examined, not input to be trusted: the screen shows declared against measured area, overlaps and dates, and declines to adjudicate them. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  2. Camposcope's design record explains why it does not use circles around a centre, as Naturametrics does: “A 40 000 ha property and a 12 ha one have nothing comparable about their centroids; ‘the first 500 m outside the fence’ means the same thing for both.” Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  3. The app refuses transitions between years less than five years apart. Over short gaps, most apparent change is classification flicker in mixed pixels — map error, not change on the ground. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  4. The line is drawn in the design record: “‘This land was already cleared in the 2008 imagery’ is an observation. ‘This is consolidated area’ is a legal classification that depends on facts Camposcope cannot see — the date of the occupation rather than of the photograph, the property's status at the time, subsequent decisions — and that only a competent authority makes.” Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  5. No code path writes a number without its provenance record. Camposcope adds the time of each SICAR query and the registry's self-declaration disclaimer, so the caveat travels with every exported file. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
